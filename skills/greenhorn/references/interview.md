# The interview

The chapters giddy-up offers, in the order they are usually recommended,
with the questions in each. How to run one (short messages, one question
at a time, follow-ups, the close) is in `giddy-up.md`, "Running a
chapter" and "The new-hire stance". Nothing here is read out to the
admin; they see the questions, one at a time, in their own language.

Each chapter says how it is asked: **plain chat** when the admin's own
words are the point, the **question tool** when the answer is a choice
with a sensible default (always with room for an answer of their own),
or a mix. It names what it feeds, the profile section its answers go
under, what it needs before it can start, and when it is done. Skip a
question the website, a document, or an earlier answer already settled;
read the answer back instead.

Only "Who we are" is needed for the first skills, and "How we sound"
for the voice and the persona among them. The confirmations card
(`giddy-up.md`, "The confirmations card") comes before the first skills
are written. "Our AI house rules" and "AI in our daily work" are needed
before anything goes to the whole team. Everything else is when the
admin likes. The
chapters below are in the recommended order, each one using what the
one before produced; the reasoning is in `giddy-up.md`, "Choosing what
to do today".

## Who we are

**Recommended first.** Plain chat throughout, as a brainstorm: quick
questions, building on answers, going deeper while it keeps yielding.
**Feeds:** compass, and the values half of voice. **Profile:** Company
compass; Values and voice. **Needs:** the website address
(`giddy-up.md`, "The website and a public search" runs first as the
warm-up). **Takes:** half an hour to forty-five minutes.

Who we are:

- Start from the first guess drawn from the website and the search:
  "Here's what I think I found. What did I get wrong?" Then: what does
  the company do, in one paragraph a new hire would understand?
- Who are your customers or the people you serve, in two or three groups?
- What do people most often get wrong about you? This becomes "what we
  are not", the most useful part of the compass. Follow up on each one:
  where does the confusion come from?
- Which public links should the assistant point people at: the website,
  support, policies, a customer guide?
- Who else does what you do? Two to four names, and in one line each
  how you differ from them, in the admin's words. Handle with care:
  the names go into the profile so the assistant recognises a competitor
  in a customer's question or a market-watch task, and the differences
  feed the compass's "what we are not"; nothing about how those
  companies work or sound shapes how this company is described. Keep
  the names and the differences; drop the rest. If the admin would
  rather not name anyone, skip it.
- **The unwritten part.** "What do you know about working here that I
  could never read on your website or anywhere public, but that I
  have to get right on any piece of work?" Ask it plainly, then stay
  on it: the first answer is usually a rule ("we never quote before the
  workshop has seen the drawings"), the second a relationship or a sensitivity, the
  third a piece of history that explains both. Each answer goes to its
  home: a conduct essential, a compass "what we are not", a fact in a
  skill, or a candidate skill. This is the question the whole interview
  exists to ask; give it time.

What we stand for, when a brand guide or values document exists: mine it
first, and turn these into confirming what was found. Otherwise, the
questions a brand agency would ask, one at a time, with follow-ups:

- What does the company exist to change for its customers?
- What does it refuse to be, even when that would be easier or pay
  better?
- How does a customer describe you to a friend after a good experience?
  And after a bad one?
- Which competitor would you least like to sound like, and why?
- Which words do colleagues use about themselves and their work?
- From the answers, propose three to five values the company actually
  acts on. For each: how does it sound when done well, and what does it
  sound like when overdone? The admin edits the pairs.
- When must the assistant be sober rather than warm? Bad news, incidents,
  billing disputes, and legal matters are the usual list; ask what the
  company's version is.
- Is the company open about using AI, with customers and with each
  other? The honesty rule needs to know.

**Enough when:** the one-paragraph description is agreed; "what we are
not" has two or more items; three to five values each have their
sounds-like and never-sounds-like pair; the sober-register list exists;
and the admin's answers have started to repeat. Then reflect the whole
back as a short portrait, one paragraph, and let them edit it. Stop
there; the voice is the next chapter, and it starts from real writing.

## Our documents

**Recommended second, as a first pass of ten minutes**, and again
whenever more arrives: every document removes questions elsewhere, the
voice needs the sent writing, and the portfolio needs the brochures and
offers. Plain chat for the list, then the admin drops files into
`sources/`. **Feeds:** whatever each document covers. **Profile:**
the chapter each fact belongs to, plus Intake notes. **Needs:** the gate
question first (`giddy-up.md`, "Documents and intake"), and the documents
at hand. **Takes:** depends on the pile; long documents can be summarised
by a helper while the conversation goes on.

- Which documents exist: a brand guide, a values statement, a staff
  handbook, a style guide, a glossary, an onboarding deck, a customer
  handbook, an AI or data policy?
- Can you share three or four real emails, a quote or proposal, a
  customer reply, and an internal update you are proud of? And one or
  two you wince at? These make the voice skill real.
- Is there material some colleagues must not see, such as HR, payroll,
  legal, or board papers? Usually that material is on the refusal list
  anyway. If something must go in but only for some people, explain what
  the plan can protect (`references/conventions.md`, "Audiences: one tier by default") with the
  question tool: a separate restricted plugin, shared with named people
  only, or left out.

The per-document steps are in `giddy-up.md`, "Intake, per document". A
document the admin has to ask someone else for goes under Open items.

**Templates from the examples.** When two or more sent examples of the
same kind come in (quotes, order confirmations, delay notices, status
reports, proposals), offer to distil a baseline: the structure and the
fixed parts every version shares, with the fill-in parts marked. Ask
only about substance ("these three always say the price holds for
thirty days; is that a rule or a habit?"); the shape is the
assistant's. Approved baselines become the templates skill, one
reference file per document kind, usable company-wide as the starting
point for every such document from then on. The sent examples
themselves stay in `sources/`: they were written for one customer and
never ship. One example is not enough
to distil from; note the kind under Open items and ask for more.

**Enough when:** every document in the folder has a fate recorded, and
the missing ones are open items.

## How we sound

**Recommended third, right after the documents' first pass and before
the first skills are written.** The voice is distilled from how the company actually writes, never designed from
the values alone: a voice built from values and good intentions comes
out as the assistant's own default register, which is exactly the
polished, over-helpful one the voice skill exists to prevent. Plain
chat, with two exceptions that use the question tool: the register pick
below, each sample as its option's preview, and the persona's
name-and-role card at the end. **Feeds:** voice and persona.
**Profile:** Values and voice; Persona. **Needs:** "Who we are" done,
since the values and the persona's behaviours come from it; the gate
question asked (`giddy-up.md`, "Documents and intake"), since the
samples are documents; and three to six real
pieces of the company's writing in `sources/`: emails that were sent, a
quote or proposal, a customer reply, an internal update; the ones the
admin is proud of, and one or two they wince at. Ten minutes in a sent
folder usually finds them. **Takes:** forty-five minutes to an hour.

Without samples, offer first to collect them now: give the full path
to `sources/`, say what to look for, ask the gate question if it has
not been asked, and wait while the admin drops them in. If the admin
has none at hand or would rather not, park the chapter. The voice and
the persona wait together, since the persona takes its words and the
honesty rule from the voice. Say why in one sentence, note under Open
items what to collect, and go back to choosing a chapter; once "Who we
are" is done, the other first skills are written without these two.
The rule in `giddy-up.md`, "Enough to write", applies to the voice more
than to any other skill.

1. **Mine the samples first**, before asking anything: how they open and
   close; how long a reply runs; how formal; how they say no, how they
   handle bad news, how they ask for something; what they never say;
   which words come back; what the wince-at ones do that the proud ones
   do not. Read the findings back as "this is how you sound, as far as I
   can tell", for correction. The sent pieces outrank anything said
   about the voice in the abstract.
2. **Connect to the values** from "Who we are": which of the pairs the
   samples already live up to, and where the writing and the values
   disagree. Ask which should win; usually the samples, sometimes a
   value the company wants to grow into.
3. **Tune by example.** Take one real everyday piece and rewrite it in
   two or three candidate registers that fit the findings, labelled
   plainly ("warm and direct", "friendly but formal", "brisk"); let the
   admin pick with the question tool, then ask what is off in the
   winner and rewrite until it lands. Two or three passes is normal.
   Repeat once with a sober piece (a delay or bad-news note) and once
   with an internal update if colleagues write many. The chosen samples,
   with their rejected alternatives and their labels, go into the
   profile under Values and voice as they are chosen, and become the
   voice skill's before-and-after examples when it is written, recast
   in the agreed placeholder names; the chosen labels set its register
   dial.
4. **The quiet rule and the tells.** Ask whether there is punctuation or
   phrasing the company bans or insists on. The de-AI pass bans em
   dashes and the usual tells by default, and above all the maximally
   cooperative register: spelling out every step, offering more help at
   the end, asking the reader for what the writer could have looked up,
   polishing every sentence until it is complete and balanced. The
   default is to say as much as the reader needs and no more, and to
   leave unsaid what they can infer. Confirm that default, or hear
   where the company wants it changed.

Say why this matters, once: the voice skill shapes every piece the
assistant writes for anyone on the team, so the time spent here is the
best-spent time of the whole setup.

**The persona**, last in the chapter. How the assistant comes across
when it speaks as itself, or as the company: the guideline the voice
skill does not carry, because voice is for the words a person sends.
Written for every company, in two parts the admin confirms and one they
choose:

- **How it comes across.** The values from "Who we are" as the
  assistant's behaviour when it speaks as itself: for each value, what
  it does and what it never does, read back as a short table.
- **The introduction.** One paragraph in the first person, written with
  the admin: an AI and says so, what it was taught, what it is here
  for, how to brief it. The admin strikes any line.
- **A name and a role, optional.** One card, "no" as the default: some
  companies want their assistant to have a name, and perhaps a role
  ("the office's writing desk", "the first stop for a product
  question"), so colleagues meet the same colleague in the handout, in
  a chat window, and in an opening message; others would rather not
  personify an AI, and that is a fine answer. On a yes: two or three
  name suggestions from the company's own words, the trade, or the
  place, never a human first name, since the assistant never presents
  as a person (the honesty rule in the voice skill); the admin picks or
  brings their own, and says in a line what it means to them and what
  the role is. A human first name is declined whoever proposes it, in
  one sentence with two alternatives ("I'd rather not carry a person's
  name, since I'm not one; how about Ledger, or Workbench?"). The skill
  then carries both, the assistant answers to
  the name, and the skill's description triggers on it.

These go into the profile under Persona and become the persona skill
(`giddy-up.md`, "What each chapter unlocks"); campfire uses the
introduction as written.

**Enough when:** the samples are mined and the read-back is corrected;
one everyday sample and one sober sample have been chosen and tuned;
the tells and the quiet rule are confirmed; the admin recognises the
company in a one-paragraph portrait of how it sounds; the persona's
behaviours and introduction are confirmed; and the name-and-role card
has been answered either way.

## What we offer, and how it gets done

**Recommended fourth, after the first skills.** The portfolio: each
thing the company offers,
with enough context and examples that the assistant can talk about it
the way a colleague would, and the internal flow that delivers it, which
is what the teams actually work with day to day. Plain chat for the
story, the question tool for sorting ("which of these are the main
ones?"). **Feeds:** compass (the portfolio, in depth), customer-process
where a customer-facing document exists, and the candidate skills under
Open items (one per internal flow worth its own skill). **Profile:** What
we offer. **Needs:** nothing to start; brochures, offers, price lists,
process diagrams, and tool screenshots in `sources/` make it much
better, and go through intake first. **Takes:** half an hour to an hour,
more for a wide portfolio.

Drill to the level the company needs: one service deep for a
one-service company; an overview plus the two or three biggest for a
wide portfolio, with the rest named and parked.

- What do you offer, as a list, in the words you use with customers?
  Which are the main ones by revenue or by volume, and which are
  occasional?
- For each main one: what is it, for whom, what does it include and
  what does it not, what does a typical case look like from start to
  finish, and what do people most often misunderstand about it? Ask for
  a real (scrubbed) example of an offer or a delivered piece of work.
- How does each one get done inside: who is involved, in which order,
  which tools or systems, where does it usually go wrong, and what does
  a new colleague get wrong the first time? This is the internal flow,
  and the part the assistant will be asked about most by the team.
- For each main one, the unwritten part again, narrowed: "What about
  this one is nowhere in the brochure or the handbook, but everyone who
  does it knows?" The exceptions, the customer who is handled
  differently and why, the step that is skipped in practice, the number
  that is never said out loud. Scrubbed where it names people.
- Which of these flows produce writing every week (offers, order
  confirmations, status updates, reports)? Each one is a **candidate
  skill**: note what it would do, where the examples are, and who knows
  the flow, under Open items. Built later through wrangle, not here.
- Is there a customer-facing description of the process (a handbook, a
  service description, terms)? If so, the "How we work with customers"
  chapter uses it as its source.

Only when the admin brings it up, never as a question from the
assistant: a mailbox or shared drive the session has a connector for.
Sent offers, order confirmations, and replies tell the portfolio story
and the voice better than any brochure. The rules for reading a sample
are in `giddy-up.md`, "Connected sources":
the admin's explicit say-so, the house rules or the gate question
allowing it, a stated period and count, nothing copied into the folder
except scrubbed excerpts the admin approves.

**Enough when:** every main offering has its what, for whom, what it
excludes, a typical case, and its internal flow in the profile; the
occasional ones are named; and every weekly-writing flow is a candidate
skill under Open items.

## AI in our daily work

The chapter that makes the harness useful on day one, not just correct.
Plain chat, with a question card or two for sorting. The more concrete
the answers, the better the first skills land, so go for real cases and
real wording rather than general impressions. **Feeds:** conduct's
essentials, working-with-ai, the first skills' examples, the open items
(candidate skills), and the day-one list that the admin's own trial
tests. **Profile:** Where AI already helps; Systems and conduct; Working
with AI. **Needs:** nothing; a few recent AI conversations the admin can
paste or drop into `sources/` help a lot. **Takes:** thirty to sixty
minutes.

What people work on and what they already do with AI:

- Who are the main groups of colleagues, and what does each spend its
  week on? Two or three lines per group; this is where the assistant's
  first users come from.
- Where does AI already help today, and who uses it? Which tools, for
  what, how often? Which prompts, templates, or saved instructions do
  people already reuse? Ask for them; a prompt someone reuses every week
  is a skill waiting to be written down.
- **No AI use today?** Then ask where it would help: which tasks are
  repetitive, and which are done well today by people who carry a lot
  in their heads (the inputs they need, the specs, the terminology, the
  checks they run without thinking)? Those tasks are the best first
  candidates, because the knowledge exists and only has to be written
  down. Who are those people, and would they spend half an hour with the
  admin, or with the assistant, to get it out?
- Which writing comes back every week: offers, quotes, reports, tender
  answers, customer updates? Which of those would you like the assistant
  to take on first? For each candidate, note what it would do, what
  material exists (templates, good examples, a tool that already helps),
  and who knows the flow. These are **candidate skills**: written down
  under Open items, built later through wrangle, never in this chapter.
  Say so: "Noted. Once the basics are in, this is one of the first things
  we build."

What AI gets wrong today:

- Think of the last three to five times AI got something wrong here, or
  gave an answer a colleague had to redo. For each: what was asked, what
  came back, what the right answer was, and why the difference matters.
  Ask for the actual exchange when it exists. Each case becomes one of
  three things, and the assistant says which: a rule in conduct's
  essentials (it must never do this), a fact in a skill (it did not know
  this), or a candidate skill (the whole task needs teaching). Record
  the case and its destination in the profile; these are the first
  before-and-after examples the skills carry, scrubbed.
- What does a confident but wrong answer cost in your line of work, and
  what should people always check before trusting one? This becomes the
  check-before-trusting table in working-with-ai.
- What are the five things the assistant must never get wrong here? Often
  the cases above already answer it; where they do not, ask, and follow
  up on each: what happened the last time someone got it wrong?

The frame it runs in:

- Which systems, if any, will people connect to the assistant? Which stay
  unconnected?
- Where do people use the assistant: the desktop app, the web, a phone?
  It decides what the skills can rely on.
- Which phase is the company in with AI today, and which does it want to
  reach this year: curious (a smart assistant that answers and drafts),
  enabled (a thinking partner on analysis and decisions), or native (it
  handles repetitive work in the background with people owning the
  judgment)? One card, the three options with a line each. The handout
  sets expectations from the answer (`working-with-ai-craft.md`, "The
  three phases").
- For each main group of colleagues, what will they ask the assistant
  first, and what must be right in that answer for them to come back a
  second time? This is the **day-one list**: written in the profile
  under Working with AI, used as the test cases for the admin's own
  trial (`giddy-up.md`, "Trying it yourself, then the team") and, later,
  as the examples in the staff handout.

**Enough when:** each main group has its week and its first ask on
record; three to five wrong-answer cases each have a destination; the
never-get-wrong list has three to five items with a story each; the
check-before-trusting list exists; every reused prompt and every
weekly-writing flow is a candidate skill under Open items; and the
day-one list has one line per group.

## Our words

Optional, quick, and mostly confirmation by the time it comes: the terms
have been piling up since "Who we are". Plain chat. **Feeds:** glossary. **Profile:**
Vocabulary. **Needs:** nothing. **Takes:** ten to twenty minutes.

- Which terms, abbreviations, and codes do colleagues use every day that
  an outsider would not know? Rapid fire; a list is fine here.
- Which of those do people, or customers, get wrong?
- Are there reference numbers people quote, like order, ticket, or
  invoice numbers? What do they look like? Should the assistant always use
  those rather than the long internal ids a system might show?

**Enough when:** the list has stopped growing and each term has a
one-line meaning.

## Our look

Optional, offered actively: on-brand documents and slides are one of the
first things colleagues notice. A mix: the admin drops files in
`brand-sources/` and answers in chat; the assistant works the values out
and confirms each with the question tool or a mockup. Asked in the
admin's terms, never a designer's. **Feeds:** brand-assets. **Profile:**
Brand. **Needs:** anything that shows the look: logo files, a photo of
the letterhead, a screenshot of the website, a brand PDF. **Takes:**
half an hour.

- What do you have of your logo? Any form helps. Which version is used
  where, and are there rules about backgrounds or spacing?
- Which colours are yours? Take them from the logo, the website, or a
  shared image, and show them as swatches with their hex values for a yes
  or no. Nobody is asked for a hex value.
- Which fonts do you use? By name from memory, from the brand guide, or
  identified from the website's stylesheet. Then the licence check in
  `giddy-up.md`, "Audience and brand values".
- Is there a house style for documents and slides? If not, propose a
  default look from the palette and fonts as a small mockup of a document
  header and a slide, and confirm it.

**Enough when:** the palette, the fonts, the logo files that may ship,
and one agreed sample look are confirmed.

## Our AI house rules

Required before the team publish, not before the first skills. Often
needs a director, legal, or a works council within reach; park it until
then if needed. Mostly the question tool, with the usual options
offered as prompts rather than defaults, and plain chat for the reasons.
**Feeds:** ai-house-rules (`metadata.role: house-rules`) and Layer B of
the always-on block. **Profile:** AI house rules. **Needs:** whoever must
agree the rules, or the admin's authority to set them. **Takes:** half an
hour, plus whatever sign-off takes.

- Which information may never go into an AI at all? Start from the gate
  question's answer if it was asked; complete it. Candidates, as two
  multi-select questions on one card (`giddy-up.md`, "Documents and
  intake"): customers'
  personal data, payroll and staff records, legal matters, unreleased
  financial figures, source code, anything a customer marked
  confidential.
- How may people work with company systems through the assistant: only
  through connectors the company provides, on the systems' own screens
  with the person driving, or not yet at all? Is a script that uses
  someone's own login ever acceptable? The usual answer is no, and it is
  better said before anyone tries.
- What must a person always confirm before it happens: sending,
  committing money, creating or changing records, publishing?
- How open is the company about using AI: with customers, with each
  other, in what it sends? Start from the answer in "Who we are" if it
  was given. This is the only part of the honesty rule that is the
  company's to choose. The assistant never presents itself as a person,
  whatever the answer; that is the honesty rule in the voice skill, and
  it is not offered as a choice here or anywhere.
- When the assistant cannot do something someone needs, who do they
  tell? That becomes the feature-request route: the advocate and the
  shared channel, not a ticket queue.
- Is there an existing AI or data policy? If so, it is read now, through
  intake, and the house rules sit under it rather than restate it.
- Has this been agreed with whoever must agree it, or does it still need
  to be? An unagreed rule set is an open item, and roll-out's readiness
  checklist waits for it.

**Enough when:** the data list, the routes, the confirm-first list, how
open the company is about AI, and the feature-request route are each
one line in the profile, and the sign-off state is recorded.

## How we work with customers

Optional, when the company has a documented way of working with
customers. Plain chat; the document, if any, does most of the work.
**Feeds:** customer-process. **Profile:** Customer process. **Needs:**
the handbook or process document in `sources/`, or the admin's time to
describe it. **Takes:** twenty minutes with a document.

- Is there a handbook or process document customers receive? If so, it is
  the source, and the questions below only fill its gaps.
- Roughly which stages does a customer go through, and which timelines
  are they told?
- Where does a customer escalate, and what does the company promise about
  responding?

**Enough when:** the stages, timelines, and escalation path are recorded,
with the document's fate.

## Who owns what

Optional, internal only, and the one chapter that waits longest: small
teams do not need it, and nobody is asked this on day one. Plain chat.
**Feeds:** who-owns-what. **Profile:** Who owns what. **Needs:** the
admin's knowledge of the organisation, or someone who has it. **Takes:**
twenty minutes.

- For the ten topics colleagues ask about most, which role owns each one,
  who stands in, and where does it go when the owner cannot decide? Roles
  first; then the current name for each role.
- May direct phone numbers or email addresses go in the skill? Default
  no; point at the company directory.

**Enough when:** the ten topics have an owner, a stand-in, and an
escalation each.

## The practical bits

Not a chapter the admin sits down for. Everything here is assumed first
and confirmed in the confirmations card before the first skills are
written (`giddy-up.md`, "The confirmations card"), or asked at the moment it
is needed. **Feeds:** `HOUSE-RULES.md`, `harness.yaml`, skill-authoring,
working-with-ai, reins, and roll-out. **Profile:** Operations; Audiences
and language.

Assumed, then confirmed in the card:

- The admin looks after the folder, alone for now. Two or more
  maintainers is the cue to mention homestead, later.
- The admin is the person colleagues go to with questions about AI; the
  handout introduces them by name.
- There is no shared channel for questions, ideas, wins, and things the
  assistant got wrong yet. Suggest making one; every place the skills
  send people for help names it once it exists.
- Everyone on staff will use the assistant; nothing is customer-facing.
  A public tier is added later if ever, under the stricter rules.
- The instructions are written in English (`references/conventions.md`,
  "Language"); the assistant answers in the admin's language by default.
- Examples use the placeholder names Tulipware and Nordlicht Optics
  unless the admin prefers their own.

Asked when needed, with the question tool:

- Which AI assistant the team uses day to day, and which plan the
  organisation is on (for a Claude organisation: Team or Enterprise), at
  roll-out. The hand-over steps depend on the answers.
- (Developers on staff, and the short name that prefixes the skills,
  are in the card too; `giddy-up.md`, "The confirmations card".)
- How often the admin expects to change things, when campfire or
  homestead comes up.
